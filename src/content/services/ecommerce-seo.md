## The problem with most store SEO

Most online stores are organised the way the warehouse is organised, not the way customers search. The category is called "Footwear > Running"; buyers search for "trail running shoes women" and "wide fit running shoes". Without a page that matches those queries, Google ranks a competitor's category, a marketplace or a blog post instead.

The second problem is scale. A catalogue of 3,000 products with eight filters can produce hundreds of thousands of URLs. Some of those filter pages match real demand and should rank. Most are near-duplicates that dilute crawl budget and internal authority. Platforms rarely handle this well by default, and the fix is a rule set, not a plugin.

## How we approach e-commerce SEO

We begin with demand, not with your menu. Every commercial query cluster in your market is mapped to the page that should rank for it. Where there is no page, we specify one. Where three pages compete for the same query, we pick one and consolidate. This is also where [trend and product research](/services/trend-product-research/) helps: demand for new product types shows up in search before it shows up in your sales data.

Then we fix the templates. Changes to a category or product template apply to thousands of URLs at once, so a single improvement to titles, structured data or out-of-stock handling can outweigh months of manual page edits. If the site has crawl or indexation faults underneath, we start with a [technical SEO audit](/services/technical-seo-audit/) so new pages are not built on a broken base.

Finally, categories need content that helps a buyer choose, and links that point to the pages with the best margin. That editorial layer is planned through [content strategy](/services/content-strategy/), with buying guides linking down into the categories they support.
